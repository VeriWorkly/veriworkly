**Yes for roles you genuinely want, no for volume applications, and the honest reason is that nobody can tell you a readership number worth trusting.** Published surveys disagree widely on readership. That is not a research controversy. It is different questions being asked of different groups, and the spread disappears once you sort out who was surveyed.

Here is the sorting, and then the part that matters more: what a cover letter can do that a resume structurally cannot.

## Why the statistics contradict each other

Search this question and you will find confident, mutually exclusive answers. Every one of them cites a survey. The surveys are measuring different things.

| Source                                                                                  | Who was surveyed                  | Reported finding                                             |
| --------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | --------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------ |
| [Novoresume, July 2026](https://novoresume.com/career-blog/cover-letter-statistics)     | 1,000 US job seekers via Pollfish | ~60% of HR professionals read them; recruiters skip at 52.8% |
| [ResumeGenius](https://resumegenius.com/blog/cover-letter-help/cover-letter-statistics) | Hiring managers                   | 83% read them, including when marked optional                |
| [Resume.io](https://resume.io/blog/cover-letter-statistics)                             | Aggregated industry surveys       | Wide range depending on seniority and sector                 |

Four problems run through these surveys, and they are worth naming because they recur across the whole category:

**The surveys ask different roles.** A recruiter screening 300 applicants and a hiring manager choosing between three finalists have opposite incentives. Reading every cover letter is irrational for the first and cheap for the second. Averaging them produces a number that describes nobody.

**Several ask job seekers about recruiters.** The Novoresume survey sampled 1,000 job seekers, then reports figures framed as HR behaviour. Self-reported perception of someone else's process is not a measurement of that process.

**Self-reporting inflates the socially correct answer.** "Do you read cover letters?" is a question with a right-sounding answer. Recruiters know which one it is.

**Almost every survey is run by a company selling cover letter tools.** Including, in the interest of the removal test this blog applies to itself, one made by a company that also builds them.

None of this means the research is worthless. It means the useful question is not "what percentage" but "under what conditions."

## When a cover letter changes the outcome

Four situations, and they are reasonably predictable.

**Your resume raises an obvious question.** A two-year gap, a career change, a relocation to a city you do not live in, three short stints in a row. The resume states the fact and cannot explain it. A screener looking at an unexplained pattern either invents a story or moves on, and the story they invent is rarely the flattering one. Two sentences of context is often the entire value of the document.

**You are a non-obvious fit.** If you clear six of eight requirements and the two you miss are the two listed first, your resume gets filtered by pattern match. A letter is the only place you can argue that the adjacent thing you did solves the same problem.

**Small company, small applicant pool.** Under roughly 200 employees the person reading your application is frequently the person you would report to. They have twelve applications, not four hundred, and reading takes them ten minutes.

**Late stage, close call.** When two finalists are technically equivalent, the tiebreaker is usually some version of "who seems to actually want this." The cover letter is the only artifact in the file that speaks to that.

## When it does not

**High-volume applications.** If you are sending thirty applications this month, thirty cover letters is four to six hours that would produce more interviews spent tailoring resumes instead. [Greenhouse's 2024 State of Job Hunting report](https://www.greenhouse.com/blog/greenhouse-2024-state-of-job-hunting-report), which surveyed 2,500 workers across the US, UK, and Germany, found 38% of job seekers now mass-apply. Mass applying and cover letters are incompatible strategies, and trying to do both badly is worse than doing one well.

**Big-company first-pass screens.** At enterprise scale the first reader is a recruiter or a coordinator working a queue. The letter is stored, searchable, and mostly unopened at that stage.

**When you have nothing specific to say.** A letter that restates your resume in paragraph form is worse than no letter. It costs you the reader's attention and returns nothing.

**Referral applications.** A referral has already done the job the cover letter was trying to do.

## What the cover letter is actually for

Strip away the ritual and there is exactly one thing a cover letter does that a resume cannot: **it lets you make an argument.**

A resume is a list of claims arranged in reverse chronological order. It cannot say "these two roles look unrelated and here is the thread." It cannot say "I know I am light on the third requirement and here is what I did instead." It cannot say "I read your changelog and the thing you shipped in March is the reason I am applying." A resume is evidence. A letter is the case built from it.

This reframe matters because it settles the format questions people agonise over. If the letter is an argument, then every paragraph either advances it or gets cut, and the standard opening — "I am writing to express my interest in the Senior Analyst position" — advances nothing. The reader knows what you are applying for. It is written at the top of the form they are looking at.

## A structure that works

Four paragraphs, 200 to 350 words, roughly half a page.

**Open with the specific reason.** Not enthusiasm. A reason. Something you could not have written about a different company.

> I have spent three years building reporting infrastructure for a finance team that outgrew it twice. Your posting describes the same problem at a larger scale, which is the part I want.

**Make the argument in one paragraph.** Pick the single strongest link between what you have done and what they need. One, not three. A letter that makes one well-evidenced point is more persuasive than one that makes four asserted ones.

**Address the obvious objection.** If there is a gap, a pivot, or a missing requirement, this is where it goes. Plainly, once, without apology or over-explanation. A screener who finds the answer here stops constructing their own.

**Close with what you want.** A sentence. No "I look forward to hearing from you at your earliest convenience."

That is the whole document. The parts people spend the most time on — the opening flourish, the closing formality — are the parts nobody reads.

## Before and after

Generic, and interchangeable with every other letter in the pile:

> I am writing to express my strong interest in the Operations Manager position at your company. With over five years of experience in operations and a proven track record of driving results, I believe I would be a valuable asset to your team. I am a strong communicator with excellent attention to detail and a passion for process improvement.

Specific, and impossible to send anywhere else:

> Your posting mentions that the ops team is absorbing fulfilment from a third-party provider this year. I ran that exact migration at Brightline in 2024, moving 40,000 monthly orders in-house over five months, and the thing nobody warns you about is that returns processing breaks first. I would like to do it again somewhere the lesson is already paid for.

The second one is not better because it is more confident. It is better because it contains four facts a competitor cannot copy into their own letter, and because it demonstrates the knowledge rather than claiming it.

## The AI problem

Most cover letters are now written or heavily edited by a language model, and the result is a category-wide flattening. Everyone's letter is competent. Everyone's letter says the same things in the same register.

Detection is not the risk. In a [2023 blind test of 1,000 hiring managers run by ResumeBuilder](https://www.resumebuilder.com/82-of-hiring-managers-unable-to-identify-cover-letters-written-by-chatgpt/), a resume-builder company, 82% failed to correctly identify every ChatGPT-written letter. Detection tools are not reliable enough to act on and few employers run them on cover letters anyway.

The risk is what the model cannot know. It does not know that the returns pipeline breaks first. It does not know what shipped in March. Asked to write a cover letter, a model produces the statistical average of all cover letters, which is precisely the document that fails to distinguish you.

Used as an editor — you supply the facts and the argument, the model tightens the prose — this is a non-issue, and the same line applies here that applies to [using AI on your resume](/using-ai-to-write-your-resume-without-sounding-generic). Used as an author, it produces the one thing a cover letter cannot afford to be, which is generic.

## Format and mechanics

**Length.** 200 to 350 words. Past 400 the reader skims.

**File.** PDF unless the form specifies otherwise, and check that the text is selectable — the same [image-based PDF failure that zeroes out resumes](/why-some-resume-pdfs-parse-as-blank) applies here.

**Addressing.** Use a name if the posting, the team page, or LinkedIn gives you one. "Dear Hiring Team" is fine. "To Whom It May Concern" reads as a template from 1998.

**The ATS question.** Your letter is stored and searchable in every major applicant tracking system, and it is not separately scored. No filter rejects you on cover letter keywords, because there is no such filter — the same misunderstanding that produces [the 75% auto-rejection myth](/ats-resume-myths-what-the-evidence-shows). Write it for a person.

**Optional fields.** Usually attach one. "Optional" is a signal about effort, not about whether the file gets opened.

## Where tools help and where they do not

The genuinely time-consuming part of a cover letter is not the writing. It is assembling the facts: which of your roles is most relevant here, what you actually did there, what the numbers were. If that lives in your head, every letter starts from zero.

The tools that reduce this are the ones that keep a structured record of your career and let a letter draw from it. [Teal](https://www.tealhq.com) and [Huntr](https://huntr.co) approach it from job tracking, generating per-application documents against a saved profile. [VeriWorkly](https://veriworkly.com) seeds cover letters from the same master profile that feeds resumes, so the facts stay consistent across both documents and you edit source rather than copy. AI drafting needs a paid plan or credits, and the free tier keeps one cover letter at a time. Its code is MIT-licensed, and it is also newer and has less writing guidance built in than either of the above.

A plain text file of your career facts does most of this. The structure is what saves the time; the tool is a convenience on top of it.

## The short version

Nobody can tell you what percentage of cover letters get read, and the surveys that claim to are measuring different populations with different incentives. What is stable is the shape of the answer: write one when your application needs an argument that a list of jobs cannot make, skip it when you are applying at volume or have nothing role-specific to say, and never send one whose sentences would survive a find-and-replace of the company name.
