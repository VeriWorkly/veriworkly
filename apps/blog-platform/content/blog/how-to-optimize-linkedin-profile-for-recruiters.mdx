**As a job seeker, you are found on LinkedIn by a search you never see. Recruiters do not browse profiles. They query LinkedIn through a separate product, LinkedIn Recruiter, and your profile's job is to match its filters.** Every useful change to your profile follows from that, and most popular LinkedIn advice ignores it.

## How do recruiters find job seekers on LinkedIn?

**They search in LinkedIn Recruiter, a separate paid product, and filter by job title, skills, location, years of experience and company. Your profile shows up when its structured fields match those filters. So the work is filling your title, skills and location fields precisely, not polishing the About section.**

This guide is for the candidate side of that search. The sections below take each filter in turn and show which part of your profile it reads.

## First, about those statistics

You have read that complete profiles are 40 times more likely to be found, that five or more skills get you 17 times more profile views, that optimized profiles get 20 times more views.

None of these traces to a published LinkedIn study. They are attributed to "LinkedIn" without a report, a date, or a methodology, and they have been copied between career blogs for years. LinkedIn does publish research and product documentation, and none of it contains these figures in a form anyone can check.

This matters beyond pedantry. Advice built on a made-up multiplier optimizes for the wrong thing: usually profile completeness as a score to maximise, rather than searchability as a mechanism to understand. So this post works from something verifiable instead: what [LinkedIn Recruiter](https://www.linkedin.com/help/recruiter/topic/a63) can actually filter on, according to LinkedIn's own product documentation.

## What recruiters are actually using

LinkedIn Recruiter is a separate, expensive product. It is not the LinkedIn you use. A recruiter working a role opens it, builds a filtered search, and works through a result set.

The filters that matter to you, per [LinkedIn's Recruiter help documentation](https://www.linkedin.com/help/recruiter/topic/a113002):

**Job titles**, current and past. Matched against the title fields, not against your About section.

**Skills.** LinkedIn has moved steadily toward skills-based filtering, including a [Skills Match capability](https://www.linkedin.com/help/recruiter/answer/a726180) that surfaces candidates against the skills attached to a job posting.

**Location**, including whether you are open to remote or relocating.

**Years of experience and seniority**, derived from your role history and dates.

**Companies**, current and past.

**[Spotlights](https://www.linkedin.com/help/recruiter/answer/a414283)**, which are pre-built segments LinkedIn calculates for the recruiter. These include candidates open to work, past applicants to that company, people with company connections, and previous engagements. Spotlights are prioritisation filters, and one of them is something you control directly.

Read that list again as a to-do. Titles, skills, location, dates, companies, open-to-work status. That is what determines whether you appear. Nothing in it is your banner image.

## The fields that decide whether you show up

### Headline

Your headline travels everywhere your name goes, and it carries the terms a title search matches against. The default (your current title at your current company) is fine if your title is standard, and actively harmful if it is internal jargon.

Weak, because nobody searches for it:

> Innovation Catalyst | Dreamer | Coffee Enthusiast

Weak in a subtler way, because it only matches one thing:

> Senior Engineer at Northwind

Better:

> Senior Backend Engineer | Python, Go, Distributed Systems | Fintech

The third version reads slightly like a keyword list, and that is a deliberate trade. The headline is doing search work first and human work second, and unlike a resume summary it has no room to do both well. Keep it truthful and readable, then let it be a little utilitarian.

If you are targeting a role different from your current one, and you can say so honestly, the headline is where the transition becomes visible.

### Current role title

Weighted heavily, and frequently sabotaged by internal naming. If your company calls you a "Member of Technical Staff III" or a "Growth Ninja", searches for the standard equivalent will not reach you.

The fix is the same one that applies on [a resume](/how-to-tailor-your-resume-to-a-job-description): keep the real title and clarify it.

> Member of Technical Staff III (Senior Software Engineer)

Accurate, verifiable, and now matching the term people search for.

### Skills

Skills are the fastest-growing part of how LinkedIn Recruiter narrows a pool, which makes this section higher-value than its reputation suggests.

Three rules. Put the skills your target roles actually name in the top positions, since ordering matters and recruiter filters lean on the strongest signals. List things you would be comfortable being interviewed on, because listing a tool you have never opened invites a question you cannot answer. And use the phrasing employers use, not your internal synonym, which is the same [vocabulary-mirroring principle](/how-to-tailor-your-resume-to-a-job-description) that applies to resumes.

Endorsements have limited value, but skills your profile can corroborate through role descriptions and projects are more credible than a bare list.

### Location and open-to-remote

A significant share of searches are geographically filtered. If your location field is blank, empty, or set to somewhere you no longer live, you are absent from those searches regardless of how good the rest is.

If you would relocate, set that. If you want remote work, set that. These are filter fields, not preferences nobody reads.

### Open to Work

There are two versions and they are not the same thing.

The **recruiter-only** setting places you in a Spotlight that recruiters filter on directly. It is not shown to the public, and LinkedIn says it hides it from recruiters at your own company, though that filtering has never been perfect and you should weigh it accordingly.

The **public green frame** is visible to everyone, including your employer, your network, and the hiring manager forming a first impression. Opinions on whether it signals availability or desperation genuinely differ, and nobody has data settling it.

If you are employed and discreet, the private version captures most of the benefit. If you are openly searching, the public frame adds reach at no real cost.

## The fields that decide what happens next

Search gets you into the result set. These decide whether the recruiter clicks the message button.

**About.** Three or four short paragraphs, first person, leading with what you do and for whom. This is the one section where writing like a person outperforms writing for a filter, because by the time it is being read, the filtering is over.

**Experience descriptions.** Two to four lines per role with scope and outcomes, using [the same construction that makes resume bullets work](/how-to-write-resume-bullet-points-that-get-read). Most profiles have job titles with nothing underneath, which leaves the recruiter unable to tell whether your "Senior Engineer" ran a team or a ticket queue.

**Photo.** A recognisable one of your face. This is the least interesting item on the list and the one people spend the most time on.

**Custom URL.** Free, takes thirty seconds, and goes on your resume.

## A realistic hour

If you do nothing else:

| Minutes | Task                                                                              |
| ------- | --------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| 0–10    | Rewrite the headline with target title and two or three real skills               |
| 10–15   | Clarify a non-standard current job title in parentheses                           |
| 15–25   | Reorder skills so target-role terms sit at the top; remove ones you cannot defend |
| 25–30   | Set location, remote preference, and recruiter-only Open to Work                  |
| 30–45   | Write two to four lines of scope and outcome under each recent role               |
| 45–60   | Rewrite About in first person; set the custom URL                                 |

That is the whole thing. The remaining features (featured sections, recommendations, newsletters, creator mode) range from mildly useful to irrelevant for inbound recruiting, and none of them affect whether you appear in a search.

## What does not work

**Posting for reach.** Feed activity and recruiter search are separate systems. A daily posting habit may help you in other ways; it does not put you in a filtered result set.

**Keyword stuffing the About section.** Recruiter filters lean on structured fields. A paragraph listing sixty technologies mostly signals to the human reader that you will list anything.

**Connection count.** Beyond the point where you look real, it is not a search input.

**Copying your resume in verbatim.** They do different jobs. The resume argues for one specific role; the profile has to be findable for a class of roles and readable by someone who has never heard of you. Consistent facts, different framing.

## LinkedIn is one of three career assets

A profile is inbound. It works when a recruiter is already looking for someone like you, and does nothing when they are not. Two other assets cover the rest, and it is worth being clear about what each one is for.

Your **resume** is the outbound document, tailored per application. Your **portfolio or personal site** is the one you fully control, which matters because [LinkedIn's rules can change and your profile is not portable](/should-you-have-a-portfolio-website). For technical and design roles it is often the artifact that converts interest into an interview.

## The short version

Recruiters query LinkedIn through a filtering product, so optimize the fields it filters on: headline, current title, skills, location, and the recruiter-only Open to Work setting. Then make the About and Experience sections worth reading for the person who clicks through. Ignore the 40x statistics, because nobody can show you where they came from.
